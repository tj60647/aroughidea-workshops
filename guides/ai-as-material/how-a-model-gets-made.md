# How a Language Model Gets Made

*This guide covers steps 1 and 2 of the [TLDR](/ai-as-material/tldr), with more explanation.*

---

## What This Is

A high-level walk through how a language model is made. It won't teach you to build one. It shows you where a model's behavior comes from, so you know what you're designing with.

Most language models are made in three steps: **pretraining**, **fine-tuning**, and **learning from human feedback**. The idea of first training a general model, then adapting it to a task, was shown early in the paper [Universal Language Model Fine-tuning for Text Classification](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.06146).

---

## Words You'll See

- **Token:** a small piece of text, often part of a word. Models read and write tokens, not letters.
- **Token ID:** the number that stands for a token. The model works with these numbers.
- **Neural network:** a large mathematical structure that learns patterns from examples.
- **Weights:** the numbers inside a neural network. Training changes them.
- **Dataset:** a collection of examples used for training.
- **Foundation model:** a model after pretraining, with broad abilities that can be adapted to many uses.
- **Transformer:** the kind of neural network most language models use.
- **Attention head:** a part of a transformer that learns which words relate to which.
- **Fine-tuning:** more training, on smaller and more structured examples, for a particular job.
- **RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback):** training that rewards the responses people prefer.
- **Reward model:** a model trained to score responses the way people would.
- **GPU:** a computer chip that does the math for training.

## Step 1: Learn How a Model Is Made

### Pretraining

**What happens:** a massive amount of raw text passes through a giant neural network. The model learns to predict the next token from the tokens before it. Along the way, it picks up grammar, sentence structure, meaning, and some knowledge of the world. The result is a **foundation model**.

**Where the text comes from.** A common starting point is [Common Crawl](https://commoncrawl.org/), a huge, free archive of the web. Nobody trains on it directly. Teams filter and clean it first:

- **[FineWeb](https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceFW/blogpost-fineweb-v1)** is built from 96 Common Crawl snapshots. Its authors report that models trained on it did better than models trained on other open datasets.
- **[RedPajama](https://github.com/togethercomputer/RedPajama-Data)** is another curated dataset. According to the project, its second version has 30 trillion tokens and more than 100 billion documents, from 84 Common Crawl snapshots.

**How big is that?** Rough estimates for a training run of this kind:

| | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Raw text | 5–10 terabytes |
| Tokens | 1 trillion |
| Characters per token | 3–5 |
| Vocabulary | about 100,000 different tokens (for example, cl100k_base, a vocabulary some OpenAI models use) |
| Time to turn the text into tokens | weeks on one computer, or 1–3 days on 32 computers working together |
| Storage for the tokens | about 4 terabytes |
| Length of each training passage | 2,000–8,000 tokens |
| In books | 10 million or more |

**The point:** raw text becomes a long sequence of token IDs. Those numbers are what the model actually learns from.

**The network.** The network is a **transformer**, first described in the paper [Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762). Its attention heads learn to focus on how words in a sentence relate to each other, and different heads learn different jobs. Figure 5 of the paper shows two heads that follow the structure of a sentence.

### Fine-tuning

**What happens:** the model trains on structured examples of prompts and good responses. It learns how to shape a response, hold a conversation, or do a particular task. **This step makes the difference between a raw text predictor and a helpful assistant.** Fine-tuning can also aim at a field (law, medicine), an output format (code, structured data), or a language.

Two example datasets:

- **Written by people:** [OpenAssistant OASST1](https://huggingface.co/datasets/OpenAssistant/oasst1), conversations that volunteers wrote and rated.
- **Generated by a model:** [Stanford Alpaca](https://huggingface.co/datasets/tatsu-lab/alpaca). Each row has an instruction, an optional input, and an output. For example:

| Instruction | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Identify the odd one out. | Twitter, Instagram, Telegram | Telegram |
| What is the capital of France? | | The capital of France is Paris. |

### Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

**What happens:** the model writes several responses to the same prompt. People, or more powerful models, pick the best one. A **reward model** learns from those choices, and the network's weights are updated so it writes more responses like the chosen ones and fewer like the rejected ones. **This step shapes the model's judgment about what a good response is.**

Example dataset: [Anthropic HH-RLHF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Anthropic/hh-rlhf), from the paper [Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862). Each row pairs a "chosen" conversation with a "rejected" one. Some rows contain offensive text, because the dataset includes examples of responses to avoid.

### Why This Matters to a Designer

How these three steps are done can produce very different models. The same foundation model can become a coding assistant, a legal advisor or a creative-writing tool, depending on steps 2 and 3. A model's "personality" is a design result, not an accident.

### Reasoning Models

Reasoning models have a slightly different step 2. They also train on problems with worked, step-by-step solutions.

- **Paper:** [Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903), which showed that step-by-step reasoning improves answers.
- **Dataset:** [GSM8K](https://huggingface.co/datasets/openai/gsm8k), grade-school math word problems with step-by-step solutions.

### How Much Computing It Takes

Training a leading model takes tens of thousands of GPUs, running for weeks or months. Meta published that its Llama 3 405B model, released in July 2024, trained on 16,000 H100 GPUs for about two months ([The Llama 3 Herd of Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)). Many labs no longer publish these details for their newer models.

## Step 2: Compare Other Kinds of Models

Every model learns from examples of relationships. What changes is what you show it and what you ask it to predict. The pattern is always the same: **collect, curate or structure, create things to predict, train.**

| Model | Training data might look like | What the model learns to predict |
|---|---|---|
| Language | books, websites, code, conversations | the next token, already present in the text |
| Reasoning | language, plus math, code and problems with solutions | correct answers, solution steps, and scores from a checker |
| World | video, simulations, sequences of situations and actions | what actually happened next |
| Embodied | video, depth, sensor readings, spatial data, robot observations | objects, relationships and situations, often from observations or demonstrations |
| Vision-language-action | camera images, an instruction, the robot's sensor readings, and actions | the action a human or robot demonstrator actually took |
